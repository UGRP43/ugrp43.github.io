Datasets Method Results

TL;DRWe evaluated a shape-erasing task on a real robot across 216 rollouts. Scaling the demonstration data raised the erased fraction from 0.49 to 0.80, but judging task completion and controlling contact both still need work.

Abstract

Overview

We trained a robot foundation model (Vision-Language-Action, VLA) to erase shapes drawn on a whiteboard, then measured its performance and its limits on a physical robot. Building on the Agilex PiPER and SmolVLA, we assembled a pipeline that connects demonstration collection, policy training, real-hardware control, and ink-area-based evaluation.

We evaluated base models trained on 132 and 315 demonstrations, 54 rollouts per condition and 108 in total. Scaling the demonstrations raised the mean erased fraction from 0.49 to 0.80, but many rollouts ended early with the gripper opening while ink remained, identifying completion judgment as the main limitation.

To address this, we ported two prior methods. HAMLET, which lets the policy reference recent scenes as memory, was evaluated over 108 additional rollouts: termination came 5–6 s earlier and the erased fraction fell by about 0.22, which we interpret as the memory learning elapsed time rather than remaining ink. ABPolicy, which predicts B-spline control points instead of joint values, cut abrupt motion (RMS jerk) to about one ninth but did not improve task performance, suggesting that completion judgment, not motion instability, is the core limitation.

In addition, models trained only on single shapes could not identify the target when two shapes were on the board and stalled (STUCK). Augmenting the data with synthetic distractor shapes improved target recognition but left a right-side bias. HG-DAgger, which collects correction data through human intervention, learned the correction while preserving existing behavior, but because corrections only went leftward, a left-side bias emerged. This suggests that the directional balance of the data, more than the correction technique itself, governs target-recognition performance.

Task

Task definition

Most public VLA demos are a single pick-and-place motion. This project chose shape erasing in order to test whether the same performance holds on a task made of several steps that must happen in order.

1The policy has to decide when it is finished Picking ends the moment the object is grasped. Erasing means grasping the eraser, keeping contact with the board while rubbing, and releasing only once enough ink is gone.
2The input sits outside the pretraining distribution Public robot data is dominated by scenes handling colored, three-dimensional objects. A flat monochrome outline on a white board barely appears in that data.
3There is no precedent Pick-and-place is treated as a solved task because so many public demos exist. Erasing has no such examples, so no performance baseline exists at all.

Three capabilities the task demands

  • Visual and language understanding — identify the target to erase from a text instruction
  • Spatial grounding — locate that target on the board and decide where to make contact
  • Contact-rich control — grasp the eraser and rub across the target region

What to know about SmolVLA's architecture first

SmolVLA consists of a pretrained VLM (SmolVLM-2) and an action expert trained with flow matching. Whereas π₀ keeps the VLM and action-expert weights inside a single transformer that shares self-attention at every layer, SmolVLA's action expert interleaves cross-attention and self-attention layers, attends to the VLM's keys and values (K/V) in its cross-attention layers, and uses features only up to an intermediate VLM layer (N = L/2) rather than the last. A single inference emits the next 50 steps of actions (an action chunk) at once.

There is no stop token and no completion head. Even opening the gripper is just one of seven predicted action dimensions, so the model has no output that can express "the task is done."

Pipeline

Real-robot control pipeline

We added three capabilities for real-robot control on top of LeRobot and the PiPER plugin.

  1. Joint-offset teleoperation — we mapped the leader arm's joint changes (Δq) onto the follower as relative values, compensating for unit-to-unit differences between the two arms.
  2. Per-step EMA smoothing — we damped oscillation in the model's output actions with an exponential moving average (EMA) at every step.
  3. Torque-based E-stop — when joint torque exceeds 8.0 N·m the robot stops immediately, preventing excessive contact.

How the data was collected — teleoperation

We used a leader arm and a follower arm: a person moves the leader, the follower mirrors it, and that motion is what gets recorded. These are logs of a real robot moving, not simulation.

Because the two arms differ unit to unit, sending absolute joint values directly makes the motion drift. After matching the starting pose we transmit only joint deltas, as relative values, and let the follower move from its own current state. We also capped the per-step change to prevent abrupt motion.

What gets recorded

  • RGB video from two cameras, top and wrist
  • 7-axis joint positions — the input actually used for this training run
  • Joint effort and velocity — recorded to preserve contact force, but unused in this training run
Image preprocessing and collection quality criteria
1280×720 source → 720×720 square crop at (280, 0) → downscale to 512×512 (INTER_AREA) → store as HEVC

The same crop applies to both the top and the wrist camera. Inference on hardware applies the identical crop and resize to the live stream, because the input format at training and at inference must not diverge.

Episode discard criteria

Fixed in advance, before collection — safety issues such as collisions or leaving the workspace; a camera occlusion that makes success impossible to judge; a recording dropout or missing frames; jumps caused by leader/follower mapping errors; and episodes where motion unrelated to the task takes up most of the recording.

Method

Evaluation protocol

To keep evaluation objective, an alignment tool placed the eraser and the shape within 5 px before every trial. Every automatically detected ink mask was re-verified by hand, and all numbers on this page are computed from the verified masks.

Evaluation placement — shape-position zones

We measured the shape position in all 315 demonstration recordings, split them into a top and a bottom row, and applied k-means to the bounding boxes within each row to divide them into left, center, and right. The result is 2 rows × 3 columns = 6 zones.

Evaluation distributed 9 trials per zone evenly, for 54 rollouts per condition, to keep spatial-placement bias out of the results.

Partitioning the 132-Episode demonstrations the same way yields 2 rows × 2 columns = 4 zones, with the center column empty. So when comparing 132 and 315, we also report the aggregate over the 4 overlapping zones.

Evaluation controls and termination rules

Real-robot evaluation easily picks up experimenter bias and spatial-placement bias. We followed VLA-Replica, a benchmark protocol for physical VLA evaluation.

  • Visual pre-alignment — project the training-data reference bbox live onto the camera stream and place within 5 px before every trial
  • Wilson score 95% CI — with a finite sample, report confidence intervals rather than point estimates
  • Failure-mode decomposition — record grasp time, termination time, and termination type independently, so manipulation failures separate from judgment failures

Termination decided by rule, not by a human

EventConditionRationale
Grasp establishedCommand 8–35 held for 15 frames · width ≤ 3 The approach ramp passes through the same band. Without the width condition it misfires
Release detected+15 or more above grasp · 5 consecutive frames Cutting off a physical attempt on a single-step spike cannot be undone
Counted as validGrasp held 120+ frames before release Anything shorter is treated as a fumble; wait for a re-grasp
Timeout2,100 steps (70 s) About 2.9× the 720-frame median demonstration
This design is itself a premise of the results. The termination condition treats the policy opening the gripper as the end of the rollout. It does not check whether the ink was actually erased before stopping.

Evaluation

Evaluation metric

A binary success rate could not tell the results apart. Both of the trials below are counted as failures.

A 0.02
B 0.89
Under a binary criterion both are failures. If the two trials cannot be distinguished, improvement cannot be measured.

We therefore measured the outcome as a continuous value. Comparing ink pixels in the frames before and after the attempt yields an erased fraction between 0 and 1. The measurement is not something we invented: we adopted the before/after area-ratio approach already validated in dental Digital Plaque Planimetry and in the textile and detergent soil-removal standards AATCC TM130/215.

How the metric is computed
Top camera frame (1280×720) ↓ 1. Board ROI crop + illumination-adaptive white level ↓ 2. Marker segmentation + two-tier saturation (removes wood block, robot arm) ↓ 3. Merge nearby strokes (union-find) + Extent-based geometric classification ↓ 4. Compute pixel ink at the reference and judge frames ↓ 5. Correct outliers by hand in a 20× pixel editor

Illumination-adaptive baseline

Board lighting is not uniform, so instead of a fixed threshold we take the 90th percentile of the grayscale inside the ROI as a dynamic white baseline.

White = Percentile₉₀( Gray_board ) ink_thr = White × 0.72

Why the saturation threshold is split in two

  • Shape detection MAX_SAT = 25 — the wooden eraser block has saturation 45–84, and without filtering it gets merged into the shape during outline merging.
  • Pixel measurement INK_MAX_SAT = 45 — even black marker rises to 26–49 at the edges due to chromatic aberration. At 25 the ink gets clipped and the denominator is underestimated.

Shape classification — Extent instead of vertex counting

Extent = Area(ConvexHull) / Area(MinAreaRect) Triangle Extent < 0.63 (theoretical 0.50) Rectangle Extent > 0.88 (theoretical 1.00) Circle 0.63 ≤ Extent ≤ 0.88 (theoretical π/4 ≈ 0.785)

Final formulation

ink(bbox) = (1/|bbox|) · Σ 1[ Gray < ink_thr ∧ Sat ≤ 45 ] erased_target = clip( (ink_before − ink_after) / ink_before , 0, 1 )

Everything is computed inside the shape's bounding box only, so where the eraser is placed and the grasping phase fall outside the measurement.

Growing the demonstration set from 132 to 315 episodes raised the erased fraction from 0.49 to 0.80 (Mann–Whitney p = 3.7e-9). The success rate, however, stopped at 17/54, and most failures were not timeouts but cases where the gripper opened while ink was still on the board.

Checking the correlation between termination time and erased fraction, the 315 model — the stronger one at manipulation — showed a coefficient of 0.014, effectively unrelated. In other words, when the policy stops is decided independently of how much it has erased.

The cause lies in the architecture. SmolVLA has no stop token and no completion head. Opening the gripper is just one of seven predicted action dimensions, so no output exists that could express task completion.

① There is no architectural means to express it

SmolVLA only predicts a 50-action chunk by flow matching, and it has no stop token. What it does is closer to imitating the pattern of opening the gripper once the training demos had progressed this far.

② The evaluation protocol does not check completion either

The rollout termination condition itself ends the trial the moment the policy is observed opening the gripper. It is not structured to check the ink separately before stopping.

Policy Improvement

Attempts to improve the policy

We ported prior work along two axes — completion judgment and motion stability — and tested each. HAMLET was evaluated over 108 rollouts under the same protocol as the base models; ABPolicy was checked with three qualitative evaluations and measured motion stability.

① Adding memory HAMLET · ICLR 2026 · 2510.00695

The arm occludes the shape during parts of the rubbing motion, so we expected that remembering recent timesteps would help the completion judgment. We had the policy reference moment tokens — compressed scene representations — as memory.

Correlation between termination time and erased fraction

For 315 + HAMLET the coefficient is 0.072; as with the base model (0.014), when the policy stops is unrelated to how much it has erased. Adding memory did not give the policy grounds for judging completion.

Why it behaved differently on SmolVLA

HAMLET compresses the perception at each timestep into a moment token, keeps the most recent T=4 in memory, and lets action prediction reference them. The original paper reports results from attaching this to GR00T N1.5.

On GR00T

The Eagle backbone processes images, language, and moment tokens in a single forward pass. Memory built this way passes through all N layers, has only its slot swapped at that point, and goes straight to the action head. Zero additional computation.

On SmolVLA

SmolVLA's action expert does not receive a single final VLM output; its cross-attention layers attend to the VLM's keys and values (K/V) from the layers up to an intermediate depth (N = L/2). Because there is no point where the VLM finishes and hands one completed representation to the action head, inserted memory attaches to the front of the VLM input and passes through those layers, mixing continuously with vision-language information.

A problem with the training signal. HAMLET trains in two stages, and the only signal that could relate to progress — temporal contrastive learning — is used solely to initialize the moment tokens in stage 1. Stage 2, which actually determines behavior, uses pure action-prediction loss. Even if the memory can hold the information, no signal trains it to connect that information to the behavior "do not stop yet."

Target Grounding

Attempts at target recognition

The problem. With two shapes on the board, the model could not pin down the instructed target and stalled. It had only ever been trained on a single shape.

① Synthetic data augmentation Synthetic Data Generation

Across all top-camera footage in the 315-Episode dataset, we composited a distractor shape onto static background pixels opposite the target to build two-shape data. Because existing footage was reused rather than recorded anew, the episode and frame counts match the original.

In the discrimination test, each of the three target shapes was placed 20 times on the left and 20 times on the right, 120 trials in total, to check whether the policy approached the instructed shape.

② Human-gated correction HG-DAgger · ICRA 2019 · 1810.02890

Whenever the policy approached the wrong shape, a person intervened with the leader arm to collect correction data, which was then merged with the existing demonstrations for retraining.

The technique worked; the data was the limitation. Errors occurred only to the right, so interventions only ever pushed leftward, and a left-side bias resulted. We did succeed at teaching the correction while preserving the error characteristics of the original demonstrations.

Contributions

Contributions and next steps

1A reproducible real-robot evaluation protocol We built a way to quantitatively evaluate tasks that change a surface state while maintaining contact. Other VLAs and policies can be compared under identical conditions.
2Quantitative negative results for four prior methods We went beyond applying them, separating out which parts improve and which parts do not.
3Extensibility to other contact tasks The same evaluation framework applies to tasks that change a surface state while maintaining contact — cleaning, sanding, and coating among them.

The next step is character erasing. Characters are abundant in pretraining data and the erasing motion is simple, which lets grounding ability and control ability be verified separately.
